There is a slide in my interest-rate workshop that no participant has ever seen. It is slide P11. It is fully drafted, and it is commented out.

What it says is that roughly one household in five fails to exercise a refinancing option when exercising is plainly worth money.

The rest of the deck teaches the opposite. §489 BGB lets a German borrower walk away from a fixed-rate loan after ten years, and the deck derives exactly when a rational borrower uses that right: when the five-year forward par rate for years 10 to 15 falls below the loan coupon. On the curve the workshop is built from, that forward sits at 3.6454% against a coupon of 3.2840%, so he stays, with 36 basis points to spare. At −36 BP he is indifferent. Below it he leaves.

It is a clean rule and every figure on the slide reproduces to the cent. We price the right as a Bermudan receiver swaption: €27,645.52 from a Bachelier closed form, €28,395.58 rebuilt on a calibrated short-rate tree. The 2.7% between them is structural, not a discretisation artefact — refine the tree and it stays.

And if P11 is right, the rule is not what happens.

That does not make the rational model wrong. §489 is the borrower's right, so a borrower who fails to exercise when he should costs the lender less, not more. Optimal exercise is the worst case, and a worst case is worth having.

What changes is what the threshold is for. "He terminates at −36 BP" is an exercise law. "−36 BP is where the money starts, and how far past it he is makes him likelier to leave" turns the same number into the main explanatory variable of a random exercise time. And a random time has a hazard rate: the chance he leaves in the next year, given he has not left yet.

One thing in Bradley Efron's survival chapter settled it. In his clinical trial the better arm was censored at 31% against the control arm's 14% — those patients survived past the end of the study, so their event times stayed unknown. Success produces missing data.

A loan book does this on every page. The contracts you never observe terminating are precisely the ones still performing. Drop them and you have fitted a model to the borrowers who already left.

One problem I have no answer to: a hazard fitted to what borrowers did is a real-world probability, and the swaption price is a risk-neutral one. Putting the first inside the second is not automatic, and I do not know yet what the right correction is.

I have not built this. The deck still teaches the rule, and P11 is still commented out. What changed is that I now think the comment marks the model rather than a footnote to it.
