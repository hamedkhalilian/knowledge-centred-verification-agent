Bradley Efron opens a chapter with a 90-year-old man doing arithmetic. 2.5% of men die in their 91st year, so he reckons he has a 97.5% chance of seeing 91.

The real figure is 84%.

§498 BGB lets a lender terminate a consumer loan after default. The research map for it runs through §491, §492, §497, §501 and §512 before it reaches any conclusion at all.

I think these are the same problem.

A loan is a time-to-first-event process. Termination on default and early repayment are two competing exits, each an option exercised at a random time. That is survival analysis, term for term:

— survival curve → share of the book still performing
— hazard rate → probability this contract exits next quarter, given it has not yet
— right-censored observation → every contract still running on your reporting date

The last line is where it stops being an analogy, and three things follow.

1. The denominator carries the whole argument.
Efron's 90-year-old made a denominator error: 2.5% is a share of everyone ever born, not of the survivors. "2% of originations defaulted" and "2% of live contracts will default" are different numbers, and only the conditional one answers a pricing question.

2. Success produces missing data.
In Efron's trial the better arm was censored at 31% against the control arm's 14% — those patients survived to the end of the study, so their event times stayed unknown. A loan book does this on every page. The contracts you never observe terminating are disproportionately the good ones. Drop them and you have priced a portfolio of your failures.

3. Validate by time, not at random.
Same data, same algorithm: a random train/test split gave 2% error, splitting by enrolment order gave 24%. The model had learned the period, not the mechanism. Any credit model validated on a random slice of its own history has that hole in it.

Where the transfer breaks, and it does: §498 is not a clean stopping time. The statute puts a procedure between the trigger and the exit — a qualified notice, a cure period — so the quantity is not the hazard of default but the hazard of a default that survives its cure window. Different random variable. I have not estimated it.

Efron was counting deaths. The statute is counting defaults. The mathematics does not notice the difference, and that is the entire point.
